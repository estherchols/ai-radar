How AI is priced and sold, October 2026.

The meter is moving up the stack, the price points have converged, and the labs just raised billions to put engineers inside customers. A working map of where every category sits, and what I think happens next.

Published 19 October 2026 · 16 min read · Prices as published on the dates cited; sources at the end

E
Esther SuebsubanantEx-M&A and corporate VC. Stanford GSB MBA/MS. My thesis work is on value capture in the agentic AI stack.
The short version

Pricing. AI applications are climbing a ladder from tokens to credits to outcomes. Customer support agents reached the top rung first because a "resolution" is attributable. Coding agents are stuck on credits, and the big vendors just made those credits weekly, pooled and tilted toward their own models.

GTM. Enterprise AI sales has split into two playbooks, lighthouse and landgrab, and the labs have decided the way to win either one is forward-deployed engineers funded by private equity. OpenAI raised $4B and Anthropic $1.5B in May for exactly that.

My view. The category that moves to outcome pricing next is coding, because the merged pull request is the cleanest outcome in software. Whoever prices it first will own the meter the way Intercom's Fin owned "per resolution."

1. The ladder: tokens, credits, outcomes

The cleanest framing I have seen this year came from Tugce Erten and Sarah Wang at a16z in August: price the layer you sell. If you sell model access, meter tokens. If you turn models into recognisable work, meter that work, usually through credits. If you deliver a business result the customer can attribute to you, meter the outcome. The mistake most application companies make is importing the model layer's cost structure into a product whose value is in the data, workflow and orchestration above it.

TokensRight for model providers. Wrong for applications, because it anchors the product to a cost curve that keeps falling and invites comparison with raw compute.
CreditsA currency, not a value metric. Good credits map to work the customer recognises. Bad credits are tokens with a different name.
OutcomesA resolved ticket, a qualified lead, a recovered dollar. Only works when the result is observable, attributable and stable enough to carry a price.

Their survey of fifty technical buyers found 27 preferred credits tied to recognisable work and 14 preferred tokens. Buyers want two things from a meter: comparability and the ability to allocate spend. Tokens give false precision on the first and make the second a forecasting exercise. The practical rule is that the billing meter and the cost meter do not have to be the same meter. Show usage detail for trust; bill on the unit the customer already understands.

Two readings of this table. First, the support category has done the hard work of defining an outcome and is now competing on the definition: Fin bills an "assumed resolution" when a customer goes quiet for a day, Agentforce bills a conversation whether or not it resolved, and the same ticket can cost between roughly $0.49 and $4.19 on Salesforce depending on how many actions the agent took. The meter is the product. Second, the coding category has converged on price points, $20 at entry and $100 or $200 at the top, but not on a value unit. Every vendor sells an allowance, and every allowance is denominated in something the customer cannot forecast.

3. Four pricing patterns from this year

The $20 / $100 / $200 ladder

Six vendors now sell an entry plan at effectively $20: Claude Pro, Cursor's old Pro, ChatGPT Plus with Codex, Devin Pro, Google AI Pro, Kiro Pro. Above that is a power band at $100 and $200. The ladder exists because the top tiers are where the margin is: Cursor's new Ultra tier buys usage at half the per-unit price of Plus, and Claude Max 20x is the same $200. The entry price is a customer-acquisition cost. The $200 tier is the business.

The pool is the price

Cursor's move to a weekly pool is the most interesting mechanical change of the year. A monthly pool invites bill shock and a support ticket on day three. A weekly pool caps the vendor's exposure to any one user at a seventh of the old number and caps the customer's regret at a week. Devin moved its self-serve plans to daily and weekly allowances in March. Expect this to be standard by next year.

Pass-through for the volatile layer

Clay's 2026 pricing memo is the clearest statement of the hybrid: fixed pricing for models with predictable cost, actual token cost without markup for expensive reasoning models, and separate credits for third-party data. Cursor's bundle does a cruder version of the same thing: first-party models in the pool, third-party at list. The principle is that you should not take margin risk on an input you do not control, and you should not hide that input behind a credit that pretends you do.

Outcome fine print is where the margin hides

Once a category prices on outcomes, the next battle is the definition. Fin's "assumed resolution" after 24 hours of silence, Sierra counting a save or an upsell as a billable outcome, Agentforce's per-action meter that lets the agent decide how much to bill: all three are ways of recovering margin from the gap between "the customer stopped replying" and "the problem was solved." Buyers negotiating these contracts should ask for the definition before the rate.

4. GTM: lighthouse or landgrab

On the selling side, the framework I now use is the one Joe Schmidt and Julian Marx published at a16z in July. Enterprise AI sales runs on two playbooks, and most founders pick the wrong one by default.

LighthouseCategory creation. The buyer has no precedent and real career exposure, so a few marquee customers have to go first. Founder-led, six-figure ACVs, three-to-six month cycles, the people who sell also deliver. Harvey needed Allen & Overy and Paul Weiss. Hebbia needed the largest funds.
LandgrabThe buyer already knows the problem and a mistake costs a bad quarter, not a career. The pitch is "I replace Y at half the cost." Demo-driven, larger sales team, forward-deployed delivery, and speed is everything because the incumbent is adding AI every quarter. Decagon in support. Stuut in accounts receivable.

Two questions decide which game you are in. How exposed is the person who signs, which rises with regulation, replacing a system of record, and output that faces the outside world. And does social proof travel, which it does in law and finance where firms watch each other and does not in fragmented mid-market categories where the controller in Des Moines has never heard of your marquee logo. High exposure and travelling proof is lighthouse. Recoverable mistakes and local proof is landgrab. When the two point in opposite directions, exposure wins.

The failure modes are symmetric. Lighthouse companies become hostages to the logo, live in pilot purgatory, and build a product perfect for one customer. Landgrab companies die of indigestion from unqualified deals and ship to five hundred customers before the product is ready. The best companies sequence: win a bellwether in one vertical, dominate it, then move to adjacent verticals that look similar. The tell that it is time to switch is buyers arriving with budget and asking for a demo instead of asking who went first.

Where this meets pricing: landgrab buyers purchase math, and math is easiest to show on an outcome meter. "We cut your cost per resolution in half" is a landgrab sentence. That is not a coincidence. The categories that reached outcome pricing first are the categories where landgrab selling works.

5. The forward-deployed money

The biggest GTM story of the year is not a startup playbook. It is the labs deciding that the last mile of enterprise AI is a services business and raising private-equity money to build it.

The logic is in the cap table. Alternative asset managers own thousands of portfolio companies. A lab that sells them a deployment arm gets preferred access to every one of those companies, and the asset managers capture more of the value from each contract than they would as mere customers. It is a distribution deal dressed as a services company.

The worry is gross margin, and the counter-argument is Workday. a16z points out that Workday went from a 54% gross margin at IPO to roughly 75% as its implementation-heavy early motion matured. Services-led growth is a phase, not a destination, if the services are turned into product. The forward-deployed engineer at Cursor already describes the job as finding the next high-value use case rather than doing the integration, because the agents do the integration. That is the version of the role that keeps margins.

For someone looking at these roles, which I am, the implication is that the forward-deployed job is becoming a commercial job with an engineering vocabulary. The scarce skill is judging which use case is worth building and pricing it so the customer renews, not writing the integration.

6. What I think happens next

Coding moves to outcomes, and the first mover owns the meter

Support agents reached outcome pricing first because a resolution is attributable. The merged pull request is a cleaner outcome than a resolution: it is binary, timestamped, and already lives in a system of record. The vendor that prices on it first will define "merged" the way Intercom defined "resolution," and everyone else will be negotiating against that definition. My thesis proposes the mechanism: a platform fee for access, a compute floor for any model, and a success fee on agent-authored PRs that a human merged without rewriting. In my Monte Carlo benchmark against Copilot, Cursor and Devin, that structure produced steadier vendor revenue and lower customer cost variance than seat or usage pricing. The sensitivity is the definition of merged, which is the same place support vendors fight today.

Bundles become the new seat

The X Subscription is the first bundle that puts a developer tool, a consumer chatbot and a social network on one meter. Google and Microsoft already bundle AI into productivity suites. The bundle is how incumbents turn a usage meter back into something that looks like a seat, and it is how they tilt usage toward their own models. Expect model neutrality to become a feature customers pay extra for.

Outcome definitions get audited

Once enough budget runs through per-resolution contracts, procurement will demand third-party definitions of what counts. The first vendor to publish an auditable outcome standard, and to let customers see the count in the same dashboard as usage, will win the enterprise renewals.

Things I am watching this quarter

  • 12 November: OpenAI's proposed date to withdraw direct model access from Cursor. The first real test of whether a coding tool can lose a frontier model and keep its enterprise cohort.
  • How Salesforce prices Fin now that it owns it. Fin's $0.99 per outcome with no platform fee was the cleanest meter in the category. Agentforce's per-action meter is the opposite. One of them has to give.
  • Whether Copilot's credit model drifts toward outcomes. GitHub owns the merge event for more repositories than anyone. If they price it, the whole category follows.
  • Migration of Cursor's legacy plans. The day it stops being opt-in is the day we learn how much developers value model choice.
