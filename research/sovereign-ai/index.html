Who gets to build a gigawatt.

Governments and sovereign funds have announced about 10 GW of AI compute outside the US and China, worth $380B at today's cost per gigawatt. About 1.1 GW of it gets switched on in 2026. This piece sizes what is actually being bought this year, maps the four kinds of state doing the buying, prices what a gigawatt costs to own in five countries, and asks what each one is worth. Every figure is dated, and the model is downloadable.

The short version

The size. Sovereign AI compute is a $21B to $77B market in 2026 depending on how you count, $49B on the average of three top-down paths. The bottom-up is $43B of all-in capex for 1.1 GW energised across eighteen programmes, roughly 675,000 GPUs, which is 87% of the top-down average. The announced pipeline is 10 GW and $380B. At 2026's pace it takes 9 years to deliver.

The shape. Four kinds of state buy four different things. Compute exporters in the Gulf pay 35% of the 2026 bill and sell the capacity to foreign labs. Industrial sovereigns in Korea and Japan buy GPUs for their own carmakers and chipmakers. Regulated builders in Europe announce 45% of the megawatts and energise 25%. Subsidised tenants in India and Southeast Asia buy GPU-hours for startups rather than buildings.

The economics. A gigawatt costs about $38 billion to build wherever it sits, because the chips are priced the same everywhere. Owning one costs $2.36 per used GPU-hour in the US and $2.73 in Germany once the German delay is counted, against $8.50 to $10.50 to rent Blackwell. With a 12% cost of capital the break-even is $3.60 to $3.76. A Saudi campus selling abroad clears a 48% margin after capital. A European gigafactory selling at European sovereign-cloud prices loses 41% after capital.

The point. The chip is 45% of the money in a sovereign gigawatt. The rest is a building, a substation and a power contract, and those are what the state controls. Time to power decides who builds, which is why the Gulf can energise a gigawatt in two and a half years and a British site takes nine. For anyone selling into this market the product is not the GPU. It is the gigawatt, financed, permitted and switched on.

1. The question and what counts as sovereign

In February 2026 NVIDIA told investors that its sovereign AI revenue had tripled to more than $30 billion in the fiscal year just ended.1,2 That is a larger business than most of the companies it sells to. It is also a category nobody defines the same way. This piece uses a narrow and testable definition. Sovereign AI compute is accelerated computing bought, financed or subsidised by a state, a sovereign wealth fund or a state-designated national champion, for the stated purpose of running AI inside a jurisdiction. HUMAIN counts because PIF owns it. G42's Stargate UAE counts because Abu Dhabi funds it. Deutsche Telekom's Industrial AI Cloud counts because the German government blessed it as sovereign infrastructure even though the money is private. Korea's 260,000 GPUs count because the Ministry of Science and ICT convened the programme. A hyperscaler region in Bangkok does not count on its own, though the Thai state's subsidy for the hours bought on it does.

Two exclusions matter. China is out. Its state-directed build is larger than everything in this piece combined, but it runs on Huawei and on whatever NVIDIA silicon clears the export line, and NVIDIA's share of China's data center AI chips fell to 34% in the first half of 2026 as Huawei reached 28%.43 It is a different market with different suppliers. The United States is also out as a buyer, because its sovereign AI spending is federal procurement inside the world's largest commercial market and would swamp the rest.

The questions are the same ones I asked of financial services. How much is bought in 2026, by whom, in what configuration, at what cost against renting, worth what to the buyer, and therefore what should the product and the selling motion look like?

2. The map: four kinds of state buyer

Grouping states by region or by GDP does not predict how they buy compute. Two things do. The first is capital, meaning the depth of the balance sheet the state can put behind a gigawatt, whether a sovereign wealth fund, a development bank or a chaebol it can lean on. The second is power, meaning how fast and how cheaply the state can energise a gigawatt, which is a function of grid headroom, permitting and the price of electricity. Put those on two axes and the eighteen programmes in this piece fall into four groups that buy differently.

Megawatts from the bottom-up model in section 5. Positions on the axes are qualitative.

Compute exporters have both. Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar have sovereign funds with more than $3 trillion between them and power that costs a few cents a kilowatt-hour from gas and record-low solar. They build beyond any plausible domestic demand and sell the capacity to foreign labs. HUMAIN's first 18,000 GB300s are for xAI. Stargate UAE is operated by OpenAI and Oracle. The US Commerce Department approved up to 35,000 GB300-equivalent chips each for HUMAIN and G42 in November 2025, with security and reporting conditions.6,7 This is the only quadrant where a sovereign AI factory is a business.

Industrial sovereigns have capital and lack power. Japan, Korea and Taiwan can mobilise industrial balance sheets at national scale, and their grids are tight and their land is scarce. Korea's 260,000 GPUs are split between a government computing centre and AI factories at SK, Hyundai and NAVER for the group's own products.17 Japan's Noetra consortium of 44 companies behind 27,500 Rubin GPUs is the same pattern.18 These states buy GPUs the way they once bought steel mills, for the industries they already have.

Regulated builders have budgets, development banks and the most expensive, slowest power in the set. The EU has selected 19 AI Factories and is tendering up to seven gigafactories of more than 100,000 processors each, with EUR 10 billion of public money meant to unlock EUR 20 billion private.11,12 France has a 1.4 GW campus planned for 2028 and the UK has Nscale's 58,640 GPUs with the first large tranche arriving in 2027.13,15 Europe announces the most megawatts and energises the fewest, and Carnegie's country index puts Germany and the UK at the bottom of ten countries on lifecycle value because of timelines and power prices.30

Subsidised tenants have thin budgets and reasonable power. India, Malaysia, Thailand and most of Latin America cannot fund a gigafactory, so they subsidise the GPU-hour instead and let hyperscalers and neoclouds build. IndiaAI offers high-end GPUs to startups at INR 150 an hour, about $1.70, against INR 210 to 335 commercially.19 Malaysia put MYR 2.1 billion into a sovereign AI cloud and froze non-AI data center applications.20,21 The state is a tenant and a price-setter, not a landlord.

4. Sizing, top-down

Three published numbers triangulate the market. None of them measures the same thing, which is why there are three.

Path A, from NVIDIA's disclosed sovereign revenue

NVIDIA's sovereign AI revenue was more than $30 billion in fiscal 2026, having tripled.1,2 I grow it 40% to calendar 2026, giving $42B, then gross up for the other accelerator vendors in sovereign deals (AMD's gigawatt with HUMAIN, Qualcomm's 200 MW, Groq's 19,000 LPUs in Dammam) at an 85% NVIDIA share, giving $49B of accelerator systems, and then gross up from servers to the whole data center at Carnegie's 64% server share of capex,30 giving $77B of all-in sovereign data center capex. At $38 billion a gigawatt that implies 2.0 GW energised, which is a useful number to hold against the bottom-up.

Path B, from the analysts

MarketsandMarkets sizes the sovereign AI market at $40.0 billion in 2025 growing at 20.6%, Grand View Research at $48.9 billion in 2026 with hardware at 48%.23,24 The hardware slice averages $23.3B. Precedence Research and SNS Insider size the narrower "sovereign AI infrastructure" market at $19.2B and, interpolating SNS Insider's $17.8 billion for 2025 and $92 billion for 2035 to 2026, about $21 billion.25,26 Path B averages those three at $21.2B.

Path C, from public budgets with leverage

Adding up the public money that is actually appropriated for compute and dividing by programme years gives $4.3B a year for the EU's gigafactories and AI Factories, $4.5B for Japan's compute budget and the Noetra consortium, $1.0B for Korea's government GPUs, and $6.8B across India, the UK, France, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia's infrastructure fund and Canada, for $16.5B of public budget in 2026.11,13,17,18,19,20 The Commission targets ten dollars of private money for every public one. I apply two, which brings Gulf sovereign wealth and Korean chaebol balance sheets in without pretending the EU target is met. Path C lands at $50B.

Three top-down paths, their average, and the bottom-up build. 2026, $ billions, all-in data center capex.

The average is $49B with a spread of 114% of the mean, wider than I would like and honest about the definitions. Path A measures what NVIDIA has shipped and booked. Path B measures what analysts will count as sovereign, which excludes most private-sector builds the states call sovereign. Path C measures what legislatures voted. For scale, $49B is 4.9% of the near $1 trillion AI providers will spend on infrastructure in 2026,28 and NVIDIA's sovereign line at $42B is 12% of its data center run-rate.

5. Sizing, bottom-up

The bottom-up multiplies the megawatts I believe are live by the end of 2026 by $38 billion a gigawatt and sums across the eighteen programmes. The reconciliation that matters

Path A implies 2.0 GW of capacity from NVIDIA's shipments. The bottom-up finds 1.1 GW energised. The ratio is 1.8x. The difference is GPUs that have been bought and booked but are not yet drawing power, sitting in warehouses, in racks waiting for a substation, or in the first phase of a campus whose grid connection is a year away. In financial services the gap between top-down and bottom-up was undisclosed intensity. In sovereign AI it is time to power. NVIDIA recognises revenue when the systems ship. The state gets a sovereign AI capability when the power is on. Everything in this market lives in that gap.

6. What a gigawatt costs to own, by country

Epoch AI's model of a one-gigawatt AI data center is the best public cost build I know. It needs $38 billion of up-front capex and $0.9 billion a year to operate, which annualises to $8.5 billion a year. Servers are $5 billion of that, 60%. Energy, the largest operating line, is $590 million.29 Carnegie's Compute Coalition adds the second half of the picture. GPUs cost the same everywhere, so what varies by country is power price, PUE, insurance and above all time. A one-year delay costs a 100 MW US data center more than $500 million over its life, more than 5% of its value, and more than doubling the power price would.30 I rebuilt the gigawatt on those inputs for five countries.

Four things fall out. First, the gigawatt costs nearly the same everywhere. Annual TCO runs from $8.67B in the US to $9.27B in Germany. A near fourfold difference in power price moves total cost by single digits, because energy is 8% of the bill in the US and 14% in Germany. Second, delay costs more than power. Germany's 1.5-year wait is worth $7.6B over the life of the asset, more than a decade of its extra electricity bill. Third, owning is far cheaper than renting. The owner's cost of $2.36 to $2.73 per used GPU-hour is a quarter of CoreWeave's $10.50 GB200 rate and a third of Nebius's $8.50 B200, and even with a 12% cost of capital the break-even of $3.60 to $3.76 is well under half the rental price.30,35 Fourth, and this is the one that explains the map, Saudi Arabia is not cheaper than the US to build in once war-risk insurance is counted, and Carnegie and GovAI reach the same conclusion for the UAE. The Gulf's advantage is not cost. It is that it can clear a gigawatt in two and a half years while a 720 MW site in Northumberland is permitted for completion in 2036.30

Utilisation reflects each buyer's reality: an exporter sells capacity it has pre-sold (80%), an industrial factory runs its own research (70%), a gigafactory serves many small tenants (60%), a subsidised pool is bursty (50%). Prices are from section 9.

The exporter campus is the only one of the four that is a business. Five hundred megawatts of GB300 racks cost $19.00B and $4.55B a year to run. Sold at 60% of CoreWeave's GB200 rate to a lab that wants capacity now, it earns $3.82 a GPU-hour and $7.0B a year before financing, enough to carry a 12% cost of capital with room to spare. The risks are the two things the spreadsheet cannot price, export policy and a drone.

The industrial AI factory is a cost centre and is meant to be. Hyundai's 50,000 Blackwell GPUs are for training the models that go into its cars and factories. The $0.89B a year is an R&D line, and the return is measured in vehicle programmes, not GPU-hours. This is the quadrant where Korea's 13 cent power and half-year delay do not matter, because the alternative is renting from a foreign cloud at $8.50 and sending the training data abroad.

The EU gigafactory cannot be run as a cloud at European prices. At 60% utilisation and 18 cent power its cash cost is $2.94 a GPU-hour and its break-even with capital is $4.39. European sovereign clouds sell Hopper-class capacity at $3 (OVHcloud at $2.99, Scaleway at EUR 2.87),33,34 so a gigafactory that tries to compete on price covers its cash cost and nothing else. It has to be valued as a research instrument that keeps Mistral, Black Forest Labs and Helsing training in Europe, and sold on Blackwell-class capability that the Hopper clouds do not have.

The subsidised pool is the cheapest way to put compute in startups' hands and the most expensive per GPU. A 20 MW air-cooled pool of 8-GPU nodes at 50% utilisation costs $3.61 a GPU-hour to run, and IndiaAI sells it at $1.70, so the state eats $-1.91 an hour, or about $-94M a year on this configuration. Against IndiaAI's five-year public commitment of more than $1 billion that is sustainable, and it buys 45,000 GPUs of access without owning a single building. The weakness is that it buys no sovereignty of supply. The hyperscaler owns the hall.

8. The solution stack and the ecosystem

A sovereign AI factory is bought as a stack, and the state controls the bottom two layers, which is where its leverage lives.

Land and power
The layer only the state can deliver. HUMAIN holds 211 plots with 14 GW of power access and signed a 250 MW JV with stc toward a gigawatt.3 Malaysia froze non-AI data center permits to keep power for AI.21 Germany's timelines are the longest in Carnegie's set. Data centers already take 42% of Frankfurt's electricity demand and nearly 80% of Dublin's.32
Ministries, utilities, sovereign developers. Nobody in Silicon Valley sells this.
Capital
Who pays. PIF (~$925B), MGX (targeting $100B, co-led OpenAI's $122B round), Mubadala, ADIA, QIA, Temasek and GIC on the sovereign side. EIB co-financing the EU gigafactories. Blackstone's $3B AirTrunk campus with HUMAIN and Brookfield's $20B Qatar JV on the private side.13 NVIDIA's own financing platforms with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman and KKR target $500B.38
Sovereign funds, development banks, infrastructure PE.
Silicon
Mostly NVIDIA, with deliberate second sources. GB300 NVL72 at HUMAIN, Stargate UAE and Nscale. Rubin first to Jump Trading and to Japan's 27,500-GPU consortium. AMD MI355X live at HUMAIN with a 1 GW target by 2030 and a $10B collaboration. Qualcomm AI200 racks at 200 MW. Groq's 19,000 LPUs in Dammam. US export approvals sized at 35,000 GB300-equivalent chips each for HUMAIN and G42, valued at about $1B, or roughly $28,600 a chip.3,5,6
NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm, Groq. Huawei in China.
Systems and operator
Who runs the hall. OpenAI and Oracle operate Stargate UAE. Nscale builds the UK. SoftBank's Noetra runs Japan's Rubin fleet. Deutsche Telekom runs Munich. YTL runs Johor. Sakura Internet took 69% of METI's JPY 72.5B subsidy pool.8,14,15,18,20
Neoclouds and national telcos. NVIDIA Cloud Partners doubled their footprint in 2026.
Models
The sovereignty argument. OpenAI for Countries puts OpenAI's models on local compute in the UAE and UK. Mistral is Europe's open-weight champion with ASML as its largest shareholder. HUMAIN's ALLaM is Arabic-first. Perplexity made its inference layer sovereign inside Germany. 84% of financial institutions say open-source models matter to their strategy, and the same logic drives states.13,14,16
OpenAI, Mistral, national labs, open weights.
Policy
The gate. The AI Diffusion rule was rescinded in May 2025 and replaced with case-by-case approvals, a rescission the GAO ruled on in May 2026. The November 2025 Saudi and UAE approvals came with security and reporting conditions. The Commission's 2027 interim evaluation decides whether InvestAI continues.6,7,13
US Commerce, European Commission, national regulators.

The ecosystem observation is that the two layers no chip vendor sells, land and power and capital, are the two the vendors have moved to influence in 2026 through financing platforms and equity stakes in operators, because they gate everything above them. The second is that the operator layer is where the sovereign premium is being captured. Nscale sought a valuation of up to $35 billion in September 2026 on the back of its sovereign capacity commitments.1 The state wants a national champion to run the hall, and the champion gets paid for being national.

Four spreads carry the argument. European sovereign clouds sell at 19% of Azure's European list price. OVHcloud and Scaleway are not winning on jurisdiction alone, they are a fifth of the hyperscaler's price for the same Hopper GPU, which is why the Commission gave OVHcloud a EUR 180 million sovereign-cloud contract.34 A Saudi exporter's break-even including capital is 35% of CoreWeave's GB200 rate. Where the margin sits

The export approvals price a GB300 at about $28,600, and NVIDIA's 75% gross margin puts its cost of goods near $7,143.6,37 The all-in capex per GPU in a gigawatt is $63,333, so the chip is 45% of the money. The rest is the server around it, the rack, the network, the building and the power plant. In a sovereign deal the state is spending more on things it controls than on the thing it imports, which is exactly why export approvals are negotiated at head-of-state level and why the Gulf's land and power, not its money, are its bargaining chips.

10. Value: what sovereign compute is worth

The value case for sovereign compute is usually made with a very large macro number. PwC's estimate that AI adds $15.7 trillion to global GDP by 2030, a 14% uplift, is the one most often quoted.39 Most of that value is consumed as software and services that can run anywhere. I assume a quarter of it depends on compute being in-country, for regulated data, defence, latency and language, and test each quadrant against it.

The macro test says India and the EU are under-investing by any measure and Saudi Arabia and Thailand are building beyond what their own economies can use. Thailand's planned 2 GW would take four years of the country's entire in-country AI uplift to pay back, which only works if the capacity is sold to the region, as Malaysia's Johor campuses sell to Singapore. Saudi Arabia's 1.9 GW would take about two years, and HUMAIN's initial capacity is reported sold out, which is the point.

The micro tests are harsher. Google's Public First study claims $41 billion of economic value from its $1 billion Bangkok region, a 41x multiplier that should be read as a vendor's number.22 Anthropic told investors it earns $1.70 of revenue for every dollar of compute, which is the best disclosed return on compute in the industry and belongs to a lab, not a landlord.1 Gartner's view of the landlords is that AI data center owners need $8.5 trillion of cumulative revenue through 2030 to cover capex, financing and a 12% return, against no more than $3.6 trillion of AI revenue attributable to them, so owners as a class cover 42% of what they need and more than $3.5 trillion of infrastructure is exposed to impairment.28

Operating margin of a sovereign gigawatt in the two quadrants that sell capacity, before and after a 12% cost of capital.

Put a sovereign gigawatt through that test. A Saudi campus selling at 60% of CoreWeave's GB200 rate at 80% utilisation earns $26.5B a year on $9.1B of cost, a 66% cash margin and 48% after capital. A German gigafactory selling at Scaleway's price at 60% utilisation earns $9.8B on $9.3B of cost, a 5% cash margin and -41% after capital. To earn its cost of capital it would have to charge $4.39 a GPU-hour, which is Blackwell-class pricing for a market that is buying Hopper at $3.

What the ratios miss. Three things do not appear above and explain why the capital keeps coming after the spreadsheet says stop. Optionality, because a state with its own gigawatt can run a national model when a foreign API is cut off. Industrial pull-through, because Korea's 260,000 GPUs are for Hyundai's and SK's products, not for sale. And bargaining power, because HUMAIN's 35,000-chip approval and $23 billion of agreements were negotiated between heads of state. These are real. They are also the reason the regulated builders keep announcing gigafactories they cannot run as businesses.

12. Synthesis

The thesis behind all of this research is that value accrues to whoever controls the scarcest input. In sovereign AI the scarce inputs are, in order, time to power, export approval and an anchor tenant. Capital is not scarce. The Gulf has more than it can deploy, the EU has EUR 20 billion it cannot yet spend, and NVIDIA is financing its own customers. Chips are not scarce in the way they were in 2024, except at the newest generation.

The market is real and smaller than its announcements. About $49B of all-in capex on the top-down average and $43B on the bottom-up in 2026, for 1.1 GW switched on. The 10 GW pipeline is nine years of delivery at this pace. NVIDIA's sovereign revenue is running 1.8x ahead of the power, which means a year of its sovereign shipments is sitting in halls waiting for substations. That is the single most useful number in this piece for anyone trying to forecast the category, because it says the constraint on sovereign AI revenue from here is grid connections, not purchase orders.

Only one quadrant is a business. The compute exporters earn a margin because they have cheap fast power, prepaying foreign tenants and the chips. The industrial sovereigns run cost centres on purpose. The regulated builders cannot cover their cost of capital at European prices and should stop pretending the gigafactories are clouds. The subsidised tenants have found the cheapest route to access and given up on owning anything.

The chip is less than half the money and the state controls the rest. That is why the bargaining happens at head-of-state level and why the chip vendors spent 2026 investing in European operators and financing Gulf buildings. Whoever controls time to power holds the pricing power. Today that is the Gulf, and the one lever the chip vendors have over it is allocation of the next generation.

What works in each quadrant. In the Gulf, sell allocation and insurance together, because the drone is now the risk that prices the deal. In Korea and Japan, sell the factory software stack, because the GPUs are already committed and the margin moves to Omniverse and physical AI. In Europe, stop selling gigafactories as clouds and sell them as the way to keep Mistral training in Europe, with a Blackwell-class price the Hopper clouds cannot match. In India and Southeast Asia, sell the certification and the national model programme to the state, and the racks to the hyperscaler who is going to build the hall anyway.

13. Limitations

  • The megawatts energised by end-2026 are my estimates from disclosed phases. Programmes do not publish commissioning dates. The honest range on the bottom-up is $35 billion to $50 billion.
  • Path A rests on one disclosed number, NVIDIA's $30 billion, and on my 40% growth and 85% share assumptions. NVIDIA's definition of sovereign is broader than mine and includes some commercial neoclouds.
  • The gigawatt cost model is Epoch's US hyperscaler build with GB200 racks. Gulf campuses use GB300 at higher density, European ones may mix generations, and construction costs vary by 20% to 30% across countries. I held capex constant on Carnegie's finding that IT is 64% of it and priced the same everywhere.
  • Power prices, PUE and delay by country are my figures anchored on Carnegie's index and EIA. The war-risk premium applies a shipping-insurance figure to a data center and is a lever.
  • Rental prices are list prices read in September and October 2026. Committed-use discounts of 30% to 60% exist and would narrow the owner-versus-rent spread.
  • China is excluded. Its sovereign build is larger than everything here and runs on different silicon.
  • The GDP value tests use PwC's 2017 figure and my 25% in-country share. They are order-of-magnitude checks, not forecasts.
