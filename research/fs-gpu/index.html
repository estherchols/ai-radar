Where the GPUs are in finance.

How much accelerated compute financial institutions buy, who buys it, what they run on it, what it costs them to own against renting, what it is worth, and what that means for the way NVIDIA should package and sell into the vertical. Three top-down paths, one segment-level bottom-up, a five-year TCO, and a value case per workload. Every figure is dated, and the model is downloadable.

E
Esther SuebsubanantEx-M&A and corporate VC. Stanford GSB MBA/MS, EIPER (AI, compute and energy). Thesis work on AI in the semiconductor industry.
The short version

The size. Financial services spends about $20.8B on accelerated compute in 2026 on the average of three top-down paths (range $15.0B to $24.3B). A segment-by-segment build gets to $11.9B and roughly 309,000 installed GPUs. Once storage, networking and the CPU estate are stripped out of the top-down figure, the bottom-up explains 88% of it. The vertical is on the order of 5.5% of NVIDIA's data center run-rate.

The shape. 58% of the installed GPUs sit with quant trading firms and payment networks, not with banks. A bank-wide assistant for 230,000 employees needs about 383 GPUs. Scoring every Visa transaction at peak needs less than one GPU of raw compute. XTX Markets alone runs 25,000. In finance the GPU is a research and training instrument first, and an inference engine a distant third.

The economics. An owned eight-GPU Blackwell node costs $4.27 per used GPU-hour over five years at 65% utilisation, against $6.99 for the same GPU rented on demand. Owning beats on-demand rental above 40% utilisation and reserved rental above 58%. Compute is under 1% of the value it creates in payments fraud and under 5% of trading revenue for a top quant firm, so the constraint on demand is not the GPU price. It is data readiness, regulation and power.

The ask. Four buyer types want four different products. Sovereign fleets want NVL72 racks in their own jurisdiction with financing attached. Regulated pods want a four-node appliance with NIM blueprints that never let data leave. Research factories want Rubin first and Spectrum-X. Renters want a reserved $/GPU-hour. NVIDIA already has the parts. The gap is packaging them by quadrant and pricing the software on the value it unlocks rather than per GPU.

1. The question and the scope

The question I set out to answer is this. How large is the market for GPU deployments in financial services in 2026, how does it split by buyer and workload, what does the compute cost the buyer under each buying route, what is it worth to them, and therefore what should the product and the go-to-market look like? "Financial services" here means banks, payment networks and processors, capital markets firms including quant trading and market making, asset managers and hedge funds, insurers, and exchanges and market infrastructure. "GPU deployments" means accelerated compute bought as owned systems or rented as dedicated cloud capacity. It does not include the far larger sum banks spend on software and services around the compute.

Two things make finance an unusual vertical for this exercise. First, it is the industry with the oldest GPU habit outside of graphics. Dealer banks ran Monte Carlo risk on GPUs a decade before transformers, and quant firms have been buying accelerators since the V100. Second, finance has turned out to be NVIDIA's lender as well as its customer. In August 2026 NVIDIA announced financing platforms with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilise over $500 billion of third-party capital for AI factories, with NVIDIA standing behind residual value for up to 25% of an opportunity.10 The same institutions that are deciding whether to buy a SuperPOD are also deciding whether to finance a gigawatt. That changes the conversation in the room.

2. The map: four kinds of buyer

Every segmentation I tried that started from the org chart (retail banking, capital markets, insurance) failed, because institutions in the same category buy completely differently. The two axes that actually separate buyers are how much compute an institution needs per site, and how hard regulation and data pull that compute toward infrastructure the institution owns in its own jurisdiction. Plot the segments on those two axes and four buying behaviours fall out.

GPU counts are the 2026 installed base from the bottom-up model in section 5. Positions on the axes are qualitative.

Sovereign fleets are the G-SIBs, the payment networks and the largest insurers. They need thousands of GPUs, they are the most regulated buyers in the world, and they are increasingly told where their data may be processed (DORA in Europe since January 2025, the ECB's 2026 to 2028 supervisory priorities, data-localisation rules across Asia). They own or they buy dedicated capacity in region, and they buy at SuperPOD or NVL72 scale through Dell, HPE or a sovereign cloud. BNY Mellon's DGX SuperPOD and Deutsche Bank's multi-year NVIDIA partnership are this quadrant.14,15

Research factories are the quant trading and market-making firms. They own the most GPUs per institution of anyone in finance, but not because a regulator told them to. They own because research iteration speed is their product, because their models and data are the firm, and because at their utilisation owning is far cheaper than renting. XTX Markets runs over 25,000 GPUs and is spending more than a billion euros on its own data centers in Finland. Hudson River Trading built a Blackwell AI factory on Spectrum-X Ethernet in a Norwegian mine at a PUE of 1.15. Jump Trading is among the first trading firms on Vera Rubin NVL72.16,17,19

Regulated pods are regional and mid-size banks, mid-size insurers and the exchanges. Their compute need is modest, a few nodes for document processing, a fraud model and a compliance assistant, but the data cannot leave the building. They buy small, pre-integrated and on their own data. The NVIDIA partner page's EDB and Cloudera entries, "without data leaving your controlled environment" and "up to 3x faster than cloud-based solutions", are written for this quadrant.27

Renters are hedge funds, asset managers and most fintechs. Elastic workloads, no residency pull, and a sharp eye on $/GPU-hour. They are where the cloud and the neoclouds compete on price, and where NVIDIA's own rental price indices (H100 one-year rental at $2.35 in March 2026, on-demand median $2.70 in June 2026) are the sales argument.10

The map matters because NVIDIA's revenue in the vertical is concentrated in the right-hand half, while most of the institutions and most of the sales conversations are in the left-hand half. A go-to-market that treats a 400-branch regional bank and a market maker the same will overserve one and underserve the other.

4. Sizing, top-down

No analyst publishes "GPU spend by financial services" as a line item, so I triangulate from three published markets. Each path starts from a dated figure and applies shares that are either published or labelled as mine. The model has every one as a lever.

Path A, from the data center systems market

Gartner puts worldwide data center systems spending at $822 billion in 2026, up 62.5% on 2025, and attributes the growth to AI servers.3 Most of that is hyperscaler capex, so I take an enterprise share of 28%, giving $230B of enterprise data center systems. Banking and investment services are 13.5% of worldwide IT ($857.5 billion of $6,369 billion),2,3 and because capital markets and payments are compute-heavy relative to their IT budgets I lift the financial services share of enterprise data center systems to 17%. Of that, I treat 62% as accelerated compute, derived from Gartner's two years of AI-led growth on top of a 2024 base that was mostly CPU. Path A lands at $24.3B of owned accelerated systems in 2026, of which $19.4B is NVIDIA-addressable at an 80% accelerator share.

Path B, from the AI cloud market

Gartner sizes AI-optimised IaaS, which is the GPU rental market, at $42.3 billion in 2026, up 96%, with 55% of it now inference.4 IDC had banking at 13.3% of global AI spending in 2024 and the second-largest industry after software.6 Applying that share gives $5.6B of financial services GPU rental. If the industry rents 60 cents for every dollar it spends on owned systems (a lever, set from the 47% of NVIDIA's 2026 respondents who run hybrid infrastructure41), owned systems are $9.4B and Path B totals $15.0B.

Path C, from the banking AI budget

IDC measured banking AI spend at $31.3 billion in 2024 with a five-year CAGR of 30% in the Americas.6 Growing it two years gives $52.9B for banking in 2026. Banking excludes the insurers, exchanges, asset managers and quant firms, so I add 45% for the rest of financial services, reaching $76.7B. NVIDIA's 2026 survey found 30% of new AI investment going to infrastructure, on-premises or cloud.1 Path C lands at $23.0B.

Three top-down paths, their average, the average restated to GPU systems only, and the bottom-up build from section 5. 2026, $ billions.

The three paths average $20.8B with a spread of 45% of the mean. That spread is not an error. Path A counts owned systems only, Paths B and C include cloud rental, and all three inherit the definitional habits of the analyst who produced the base number. Gartner's broadest AI infrastructure line, $1,484 billion for 2026, I deliberately did not use because it includes AI PCs and phones and the hyperscaler buildout.5

Reconciling the two

The bottom-up is 57% of the top-down average, a gap of $8.9B. Some of that gap is definitional. In an AI system bill of materials the GPU server is 60% to 70% of the money and the rest is network fabric, storage, CPU nodes and the data platforms around the GPUs, which the top-down "infrastructure" lines include and the bottom-up GPU count does not. Restating the top-down to GPU systems only (65% of the average) gives $13.5B, and the bottom-up explains 88% of it. What remains is either undisclosed intensity at the big banks and quant firms, or over-statement in the budget figures. Both are plausible, and I would bet on the former. JPMorgan's roughly $2 billion a year on AI with 30% to infrastructure would alone buy about 12,000 GPUs a year, which is double the fleet this model assumes it holds.

The number I would put on a slide is this. Financial services is a $12 billion to $21 billion a year market for GPU compute in 2026, growing around 30% a year, with an installed base of roughly 309,000 GPUs. It is about 5.5% of NVIDIA's data center run-rate. NVIDIA does not break out verticals, so that last figure is an inference from Path A against $89 billion of data center revenue in the quarter to July 2026.8

6. What the workloads need

Sizing from budgets tells you how much is spent. It does not tell you whether the spend is sensible or where it goes. So I built three workloads from first principles and counted GPUs. The results are the most useful thing in this piece, because they explain the shape of the map.

An assistant for every employee at a G-SIB

Take JPMorgan's 230,000 LLM Suite users, assume half are active on a given day and each uses 60,000 tokens (an enterprise analyst sits at 300,000 to 2 million tokens a month35). That is 6.9 billion tokens a day, 575,000 tokens a second at a 3x peak over a ten-hour day. A Blackwell GPU serving a 70-billion-parameter open model delivers on the order of 2,500 tokens a second at interactive latency, a conservative reading of NVIDIA's own $0.02 per million tokens claim for GPT-OSS-120B.28 At 60% target utilisation the whole bank needs 383 GPUs, which is 6% of the fleet this model assumes a G-SIB holds. The marginal compute cost of serving 1.7 trillion tokens a year at NVIDIA's stated floor is $34k. The same tokens at frontier API list prices would be $10.3M. That spread is the business case for banks owning inference on open models, and it is why 84% of NVIDIA's respondents say open source matters to their AI strategy.1

Scoring every card transaction at a payment network

Visa processes over 269 billion transactions a year, about 8,530 a second on average and 76,000 a second at peak capacity.25,26 A production transaction model of about a billion parameters costs around 2 GFLOPs per scored transaction. At peak that is 0.15 PFLOPS, and a Blackwell GPU delivers about 2 PFLOPS of effective FP8 in production. Scoring an entire global card network at peak, with 2x redundancy, needs 0.15 GPUs of raw compute. The real count is set by latency budgets, regions and redundancy, so a network runs dozens of inference GPUs rather than thousands. Training is a different matter. A 10-billion-parameter transaction model on a year of Mastercard-scale data (125 billion transactions at roughly 50 tokens each) is 52,083 GPU-hours, or 72 GPUs for a month. Retrain monthly across several models and regions, add the research that gets you to the model, and a 1,000 to 3,000 GPU payments fleet makes sense. The fleet is for the training loop, not the scoring.

A quant research cluster

XTX Markets' 25,000 research GPUs are 65x what the G-SIB assistant needs. Refreshing that fleet at today's system prices would cost $1.20B, and XTX has separately committed more than a billion euros to the buildings and power to house it. Hudson River Trading's buying trigger, disclosed at GTC, was 1.6x faster research iteration on Blackwell than on Hopper.16 The quant firm's unit of value is a research cycle, not a token.

What the three builds say together. Assistant for 230,000 staff, 383 GPUs. Real-time fraud scoring for a global network, under one GPU of FLOPs. One trading firm's research cluster, 25,000 GPUs. GPU fleets in finance are driven by research and training. Inference counts are set by latency, residency and redundancy rather than by compute, which is why inference lands in small pods close to the data while training lands in large clusters wherever power is cheap.

Three observations on the ecosystem. First, the two layers NVIDIA does not sell, the facility and the capital, are the two it has moved to influence in 2026 through the financing platforms, because power and money rather than chips now gate deployments. Third, the vertical ISVs with GPU-native risk engines (Murex, Riskfuel) are the Trojan horse into dealer banks, because they convert an existing CPU budget line into a GPU one without a new use case having to be approved.

The GTM plan I would run

Lead with the research factories and the payment networks, because they are where the GPUs are. Twenty-five quant firms and twenty-five payment companies account for most of the installed base. They are sophisticated, they buy early, and they are reference accounts for the banks that follow. The product motion is early access to Rubin, Spectrum-X design wins, and co-marketing of transaction foundation models (the GTC 2026 slate of Mastercard, Revolut and Adyen is exactly this).

Sell sovereign fleets as financed infrastructure, not as capex. The $500 billion financing programme is the most important GTM asset NVIDIA has in this vertical, because the buyers are the lenders. Package a SuperPOD or NVL72 deployment with a lease from one of the partner platforms, residual value supported by NVIDIA, and a power contract arranged through the colocation partner. The bank's CFO sees an opex line per GPU-hour, which is the unit they already use for cloud, and the regulator sees data in jurisdiction.

Ship an appliance for the regulated pods through the data platform vendors. There are 400 regional banks in the model and 4,238 FDIC-insured institutions in the US alone.13 They will not buy a SuperPOD and they will not run Kubernetes. They will buy a certified four-node box from Dell or HPE with EDB or Cloudera and three NIM blueprints, through the reseller they already use, if it comes with examiner-ready documentation. This is the volume play and it is almost entirely a packaging and channel problem rather than a technology one.

Let the cloud partners keep the renters, and price the software separately. NVIDIA should not fight AWS and Google for the hedge fund that rents 64 GPUs. It should make sure AI Enterprise, NIM and Nemotron are the software they run wherever they rent, and that the rental price index it publishes keeps the market honest.

How to read the four columns

The regulated pod is the only configuration that does not beat on-demand cloud on cost. At the 40% utilisation a regional bank realistically achieves, the pod costs $6.94 per used GPU-hour, which is the on-demand price. It wins on something else, data never leaving the building, and the sale has to be made on that. It also means the appliance must ship with a scheduler or a consumption contract (HPE GreenLake, Dell APEX) or the CFO will reject it on the numbers. Its value ratio of 15x on a single fraud use case is the strongest of the four, which is the real argument.

The SuperPOD-class system is the bank's first serious purchase and it is now the wrong architecture for its heaviest workload. Sixty-four air-cooled nodes with an eight-GPU NVLink domain is the full-size version of what BNY bought in 2024 (it disclosed "dozens" of DGX H100 systems) and what the SuperPOD reference architecture still describes. It costs $62.3M over five years and serves 1.3 bank-wide assistants, which is more inference than a bank needs. For training transaction or risk foundation models the 72-GPU NVLink domain of the rack matters more than the GPU count.

The sovereign AI factory is the configuration the G-SIBs and payment networks should be buying next, and it is cheaper per PFLOPS. Four GB300 NVL72 racks give 288 GPUs with 83 TB of memory in 72-GPU domains, capital of $6,211 per FP8 PFLOPS against $7,292 for the node-based system, and $3.57 per used GPU-hour at 75% utilisation. The constraint is the building. Liquid-cooled racks at 135 kW each need a colocation partner with the inventory (Digital Realty's Frankfurt campus advertises liquid-cooled racks for exactly this reason40) and a power contract in jurisdiction. Power and facility are 29% of its five-year cost, the highest of the four, which is why this quadrant should be sold with the colocation partner and the lease in the room.

The research factory is where the unit economics are best and NVIDIA's software is absent. Two thousand GPUs on ODM iron, in an owned building sited for cheap power, at 85% utilisation, cost $2.27 per used GPU-hour, a third of the on-demand rental price. The buyer does not want AI Enterprise and will not pay for it. What it will pay for is allocation, Spectrum-X and the next generation first. This configuration is also the one most exposed to AMD and to custom silicon, because the buyer runs its own software stack and can move.

Across the four, the pattern is that configuration follows the utilisation the buyer can sustain and the jurisdiction the data is allowed to be in. Compute density and FLOPS per dollar matter less than the two numbers nobody puts on a spec sheet, hours busy and kilowatts available at the address.

Three things to take from this. First, owning is cheaper than renting on demand at any utilisation above 40%, and cheaper than reserved capacity above 58%. A payment network training models around the clock and a quant firm running research sit well above both, which is why they own. A regional bank running a fraud model and a document pipeline sits below the reserved breakeven unless it schedules well, which is why the appliance for that quadrant has to come with utilisation tooling or a consumption model like HPE GreenLake. Second, hardware is only 54% of five-year cost. Power and facility are 24%, and software is 19%. The spread between $0.47 per million tokens owned and $6.00 at a frontier API is the economic engine behind every "bank adopts open models on-premises" headline. It is also NVIDIA's pricing argument in the vertical. The GPU is not the expensive part of a bank's AI programme. The model licence is, if the bank rents a closed one.

Where the margin sits

A B200 at $47,500 on the street with NVIDIA's 75% gross margin implies about $11,875 of cost of goods per GPU and $285,000 of gross profit per eight-GPU node, chips only.8,29 The GPUs are 90% of the node's price and 72% of its capital cost including networking and storage. AI Enterprise at $4,500 per GPU per year is $180,000 per node over five years, or 19% of five-year TCO. That software line is the recurring revenue NVIDIA can grow in finance without shipping more silicon, and it is priced per GPU today. A cloud provider renting the same node on demand at 65% utilisation earns about 39% gross margin if its cost base matches our TCO, which is why hyperscalers and neoclouds keep buying. And the GPU rental market did something unusual for a compute market in 2026. It got more expensive. H100 one-year rentals went from $1.70 to $2.35 an hour between October 2025 and March 2026, and the on-demand median from $2.00 to $2.70 by June.10 Rising rental prices move the breakeven in favour of owning, and favour the systems business over the cloud channel.

Financing changes who pays

If a sovereign-fleet bank finances its hardware through one of the partner platforms with NVIDIA's 25% residual-value support instead of our 10% assumption, five-year TCO falls by $63,000 per node, and more importantly the bank books a lease rather than capex. For the financial services vertical the pricing conversation is moving from the price of a server to a lease rate per GPU-hour, which is the unit banks already use for cloud. That is the single biggest change in how this vertical buys since the cloud.

12. Value: what the compute buys

A value analysis in finance has to be done workload by workload, because the value of a GPU in a fraud model and a GPU in a quant cluster are measured in different currencies. I built four, each anchored on a disclosed number.

Payments fraud

Visa's Advanced Authorization prevents an estimated $28 billion of fraud a year, and Visa spent more than $3 billion on AI and data infrastructure over ten years.25 That is 93x value to spend on the company's own figures, and 10.4 cents of fraud prevented per transaction. The five-year TCO of the 2,000-GPU payments fleet this model assumes is $243M. Annualised, that is 0.17% of the fraud prevented. Compute is a rounding error against the value. For a mid-size issuer the incremental case is just as clear. Mastercard's research puts the average organisation's payment fraud loss at $60 million a year.24 Revolut's transaction foundation model raised fraud detection precision by 20%.16 Apply that gain and the model is worth $12M a year to one institution, before the false-positive savings that 83% of Mastercard's respondents report. A 64-GPU regional-bank pod costs $7.8M over five years, which pays back in about 8 months on that one use case.

Enterprise productivity at a G-SIB

JPMorgan reports about $2 billion a year of savings attributed to AI against about $2 billion a year of AI investment, a ratio of 1.0x, with AI at 10% of a $19.8 billion technology budget.21,22,45 The theoretical productivity value of 230,000 users at a $150,000 loaded cost, applying the self-reported 30% to 40% efficiency gain to a quarter of their time, is $3.0B a year, so the bank is realising about 66% of the theoretical figure, which is a high conversion rate for enterprise software. The GPUs the assistant needs cost $47M over five years, which annualised is 0.5% of the reported savings. The $2 billion a year of investment is going into data, integration and change, not into GPUs. This is the use case where NVIDIA's share of the customer's wallet is smallest and where the services ecosystem matters most.

Risk engine consolidation at a dealer bank

Murex reports 7x the XVA performance and 4x less energy on NVIDIA GPUs than on CPUs.27 Take a dealer bank with a 5,000-server CPU risk grid drawing 5 MW. At 7x per GPU, 89 eight-GPU nodes replace it at equal throughput, drawing 958 kW, a 81% power reduction that lands close to Murex's 4x claim. The power and colocation saving is $14.4M a year, the avoided CPU refresh is $125M over five years, and the GPU capital required is $46.9M. Net five-year saving before counting the value of intraday risk is $150M. The real prize, which this does not price, is Riskfuel's "valuations from once a day to on demand". A desk that can reprice its book intraday takes different positions. This is the use case that converts an existing CPU budget line into GPU spend with no new approval, and it is why the vertical ISVs matter so much in the ecosystem.

Quant research

A 25,000-GPU research fleet at the owned effective price costs about $608M a year. Citadel Securities booked $12.2 billion of net trading revenue in 2025 and Hudson River Trading about $12.3 billion.44 An XTX-scale fleet is therefore about 5.0% of a top firm's revenue. Against a 1.6x research-iteration gain per generation, these firms will pay whatever the newest silicon costs, and the sales motion is allocation and early access rather than price.

The constraint on GPU demand in finance is model and data readiness, regulatory sign-off and power, not GPU price. That argues for value-based packaging, meaning NIM blueprints and vertical ISV bundles sold on the outcome they unlock, rather than for price competition on the silicon. It also means a price cut on GPUs would not materially increase demand from this vertical. A faster path through model risk review would.

13. Synthesis: where the bottleneck is and what I would ship

My thesis across all of this research is that value accrues to whoever controls the scarcest input, and in finance in 2026 the scarce inputs for AI are, in order, approved data, power in jurisdiction, and capital structure. Not GPUs. Here is what that implies.

The market is real but it is not where the sales teams think. Financial services buys $12 billion to $21 billion of GPU compute a year, around 5.5% of NVIDIA's data center run-rate, and it is growing about 30% a year. More than half the installed base sits with about fifty institutions, the quant firms and the payment networks, that nobody puts on a "banking" account list. The G-SIBs are the second cluster. The thousands of smaller banks (4,238 FDIC-insured institutions in the US alone) are a long tail that will only buy an appliance.

Inference is small and training is large, and the two want different products. A bank's entire employee assistant fits in a few hundred GPUs. A payment network's real-time scoring fits in a few dozen. The thousands of GPUs are in training transaction models, running risk, and doing research. So the sovereign-fleet product is an NVL72 training rack in a financed colocation cage, and the regulated-pod product is a four-node inference appliance next to the database. Selling the rack to the regional bank or the appliance to the market maker loses both.

Power now gates the fleets, and the quants have already moved to where it is cheap. XTX in Finland and Iceland, HRT in a Norwegian mine, Jump on Rubin. Banks cannot follow because their data has to stay home, so their constraint is a 10 to 40 MW power contract in Frankfurt, London, New York or Singapore where colocation already costs $200 to $475 per kW per month. NVIDIA cannot sell power, which is exactly why it has gone into financing the buildings. The FS vertical team should treat the financing platforms as its lead product for the top 100 banks.

Pricing power sits in software and in the lease, not in the chip. The GPU is 54% of five-year cost and under 1% of the value in payments. AI Enterprise per GPU per year is a flat tax on a wildly uneven distribution of value. I would move it toward per-blueprint pricing for the regulated pods (pay for the fraud blueprint, the document blueprint, the assistant blueprint) and toward a per-GPU-hour lease rate for the sovereign fleets through the financing partners. The quants will not buy the software and should not be asked to.

What the vendor should ship in the next two quarters. One, a Financial Services AI Factory reference design around GB300 NVL72 with a lease term sheet from the partner platforms and a colocation partner in each of the five money-center cities. Two, a certified four-node "regulated pod" through Dell and HPE with EDB or Cloudera and three NIM blueprints, documented for OCC, FDIC, ECB and MAS examiners, sold only through channel. Three, a transaction-foundation-model program that puts Mastercard, Revolut and Adyen on stage every quarter, because that is the workload that converts payments companies into owners. Four, a published GPU rental price index, because rising rental prices are the best argument for owning that NVIDIA has, and it has the data.

14. Limitations

  • Financial institutions do not disclose GPU counts. Every intensity figure in the bottom-up is my estimate anchored on a handful of disclosures. The model exposes each as a lever and the reconciliation to the top-down is the check. The honest range is $12 billion to $21 billion, not a point.
  • The top-down paths borrow analyst figures that use different definitions. Gartner's data center systems line is dominated by hyperscalers and my 28% enterprise share is a judgment. IDC's banking share is a 2024 figure.
  • Hardware prices moved sharply during 2026. B200 street prices rose from a $30,000 to $40,000 range to $45,000 to $50,000 by early October. The TCO uses $420,000 per node, which may already be low. The rental prices are from published indices rather than negotiated contracts.
  • Tokens per second per GPU and FLOPs per transaction are engineering assumptions. The conclusions about the ratio between inference and training needs are robust to a factor of three in either direction, which is why I present them as orders of magnitude.
  • I have not modelled China, where NVIDIA's share of data center AI chips fell to 34% in the first half of 2026 as Huawei reached 28%,38 and where the big four banks are among the largest in the world. The 21 Chinese banks in the top 100 are counted as institutions but their GPUs are mostly not NVIDIA's.
  • Value figures are the companies' own. Visa's $28 billion and JPMorgan's $2 billion are not audited claims and I use them as the institutions state them.
