Ten coding tools, four meters, one switch.

How ten AI coding tools charge, laid side by side. Then a 10,000-developer Monte Carlo of what happens to a Cursor-shaped vendor's revenue, margin and churn if it moves from today's bundle to a flat seat, pure usage, or an outcome-based tariff. The code and data are at the bottom.

Published 19 October 2026 · 18 min read · Prices read from vendor pages on 19 Oct 2026 unless noted

E
Esther SuebsubanantEx-M&A and corporate VC. Stanford GSB MBA/MS. My thesis work is on how agentic AI gets priced.
The short version

The category. Ten coding tools, zero outcome pricing. Everyone sells a seat with an allowance, and the allowance is denominated in something the customer cannot forecast: dollars of model usage, credits, tasks per day, or a weekly pool. The $20 / $100 / $200 ladder is now universal.

The switch. At the same starting price, today's bundle is the highest-margin meter in the set, at {{bundle_gm}} gross margin, because it refuses to subsidise third-party models. A pure usage meter is the worst on every axis. An outcome-based tariff gives up margin for retention and predictability, and only beats the bundle on gross profit once the success fee is above about $1 per merged pull request.

The point. Nobody in this category has priced the merged PR yet. The model says the vendor that does it can hold margin, cut churn, and make the frontier-model price war someone else's problem.

Three things jump out. First, the ladder has converged: six of the ten sell entry at $20, and the power tier is $100 or $200 everywhere except Google. The entry price is a customer-acquisition cost. The $200 tier is where the margin lives. Second, the meter has not converged. Four vendors bill in dollars of model usage, three bill in credits that stand for work, two throttle by task count, and one sells a flat team fee. Third, none of them price the result. Customer support agents moved to per-resolution pricing two years ago. Coding agents, whose output is a merged pull request sitting in a system of record, still bill for effort.

2. The four meters

Every pricing page above is one of four meters, or a hybrid of two. Each one decides who carries the risk when the agent loops, retries, or picks an expensive model.

The a16z framing from August is the right lens: price the layer you sell. The coding vendors are selling implemented changes and billing for the ingredients. The question the rest of this page tries to answer with numbers is whether that is a mistake they can afford.

3. The switch model

I built a Monte Carlo of a Cursor-shaped vendor: ten thousand individual developers, thirty-six months, four pricing regimes. The point is not to forecast Cursor's P&L. It is to see which direction each metric moves when only the meter changes.

The population

SegmentShareAgent usage per month, at model list priceMerged agent-authored PRs per monthWillingness to pay per month
Light45%$84$34
Daily40%$3518$70
Heavy15%$15050$240

Within each segment, usage and willingness to pay vary by a lognormal with 35% spread, and each developer's usage moves month to month by another 45%. Model list prices move each month by a 20% lognormal shock, which is my stand-in for frontier price cuts and the occasional expensive new model. The usage figures are anchored on Cursor's own guidance for its legacy plans, which said daily agent users typically spent $60 to $100 a month and power users passed $200.

Costs

First-party model usage costs the vendor 35% of list, because it owns the inference and can route and cache. Third-party usage costs 100% of list. Hosting, support and payments cost $4 per active user per month. When model choice is not penalised, 55% of usage goes to third-party models; under the bundle, where third-party costs extra, that share falls to 30%.

Churn

Every developer churns at a background 2% a month. On top of that, 15 points of extra churn per 100% that the bill exceeds willingness to pay, and 4 points of extra churn in any month the bill jumps more than 30% on the previous one. The second term is the cost of unpredictability, and it is the thing usage pricing is accused of.

The four regimes

RegimeRule
Flat seat$40 a month, any model, fair-use throttle at $200 of list usage.
Bundle (today)Cursor's Premium tier as published: $30, a $40 monthly pool of first-party usage, first-party overage at list, third-party at list from the first request.
Pure usageNo fee. Every dollar of list usage billed at a markup, calibrated to {{usage_markup}} so the regime starts at the same revenue per user as the bundle.
Three-part tariff$15 platform fee, a $10 compute floor of any-model usage included, usage above the floor at 1.1x list, and a success fee per merged agent-authored PR, capped at $150 a month. 85% of merged PRs pass the "agent-authored, merged, lightly edited" test. The fee is calibrated to {{pr_fee}} so the regime also starts at the bundle's revenue per user.
Why calibrate

If I let the tariff charge more than the bundle in month one, the model would just tell me that charging more makes more money. Calibrating every regime to the same month-one revenue per user, {{target_arpu}}, isolates the effect of the structure. Section 5 then relaxes the calibration and lets the success fee move.

Four things the table says, in order of how much they surprised me.

  • The flat seat is underwater. {{seat_gm}} gross margin before any sales or R&D cost. This is the pricing Cursor ran until June 2025, and the model reproduces why it stopped: 15% of developers consume more model time than the other 85% combined, and a flat price lets them.
  • The bundle is the best margin design in the set, at {{bundle_gm}}. Not because it charges more, but because it moves third-party cost onto the customer and routes the rest to first-party models the vendor serves at 35% of list. The cost is churn among heavy third-party users, who are precisely the ones who built the product's reputation.
  • Pure usage is worst on every axis that matters. {{usage_gm}} margin, {{usage_churn}} monthly churn, {{usage_billcv}} bill volatility. The markup needed to match the bundle's revenue is {{usage_markup}}, which heavy users will not pay for long, and the revenue line inherits every model price cut.
  • The tariff buys retention with margin. At equal starting price it keeps {{tariff_ret}} of developers at month 36 against the bundle's {{bundle_ret}}, with half the bill volatility of pure usage. But its gross margin is {{tariff_gm}}, below the bundle, because the compute floor and the thin markup put third-party cost back on the vendor's side of the ledger.

So at the calibrated price, the honest answer is that the outcome meter is not free money. It trades the bundle's margin for a stickier customer. Whether that trade is worth making depends on the one number calibration pinned down: the success fee.

5. What the success fee does

The calibrated fee was {{pr_fee}} per merged PR, which is almost nothing. A daily developer in the model produces 18 merged agent-authored PRs a month and values each at far more than that. So the next question is what happens when the fee moves and everything else stays fixed.

Three-part tariff only. Platform fee, floor and markup held at $15, $10 and 1.1x. Gross profit is cumulative over 36 months; retention is the share of the starting cohort still paying at month 36.

Gross profit climbs with the fee and then flattens, peaking near {{best_fee}} per PR at {{best_fee_gp}} over 36 months. Retention falls the whole way, from {{tariff_ret}} at the calibrated fee to {{hi_fee_ret}} at {{hi_fee}}. The curve says three things:

  • Above about $1 per PR, the tariff beats the bundle on 36-month gross profit ({{bundle_gp}}) while still retaining a comparable share of developers. That is the regime a pricing team should actually compare against the bundle, not the calibrated one.
  • Between $1.50 and $2.50 the tariff earns more than the bundle and keeps its margin above the bundle's, because the success fee is nearly pure margin: it costs nothing to serve.
  • Past $3 the model is harvesting the cohort. Gross profit still rises in a 36-month window because heavy users pay up to the cap before leaving, but a vendor that watches retention collapse to {{hi_fee_ret}} is reading the chart wrong. The right fee is where the profit curve bends, not where it peaks.

For scale, a merged agent-authored PR at $2 is roughly the cost of ten minutes of a US developer's loaded time. The customer's value per PR in the model is forty dollars and the fee never gets near it. The constraint on the fee is not value. It is the $20 anchor every competitor in section 1 has set.

6. Model cost shocks and strict definitions

When model prices move

I reran all four regimes with the monthly model-price shock at zero, 20% and 40%. The flat seat does not care, because its revenue is fixed and its margin is already gone. The bundle's revenue volatility rises from {{bundle_revcv}} to {{shock_bundle_cv}}, pure usage from {{usage_revcv}} to {{shock_usage_cv}}, the tariff from {{tariff_revcv}} to {{shock_tariff_cv}}. The tariff is the regime whose revenue is least coupled to the token price, which is the whole argument for pricing the layer you sell: when the model gets cheaper, a usage vendor's revenue falls, a tariff vendor's margin rises.

When "merged" gets strict

The fraction of merged PRs that count as agent-authored and lightly edited is the number the support vendors fight about, and it is where I expected the tariff to be fragile. At the calibrated fee it barely matters: gross margin moves from {{strict_lo_gm}} with half of PRs counted to {{strict_hi_gm}} with all of them. At higher fees the sensitivity grows, because more of the revenue sits on the success fee. The practical implication is that a vendor should launch with a strict definition and a modest fee, then loosen the definition before it raises the fee. That is the opposite of what Fin and Agentforce did in support, where loose definitions came first and the arguments followed.

7. What I would tell the pricing team

Recommendation

Keep the bundle for the pool. Add the success fee on top of it. The model's two best regimes are the bundle's cost structure and the tariff's retention. They are not mutually exclusive. A $30 tier with a first-party pool, third-party at list, and a $1.50 fee per merged agent-authored PR with a $100 cap would have the bundle's margin on usage and the tariff's alignment on output.

Publish the definition before the price. Agent-authored, merged within 14 days, under 20% human edits by line. Show the count next to usage in the same dashboard. The vendor that defines "merged" first owns the meter the way Intercom owned "resolution."

Treat the fee as the hedge against the model price war. Every regime in section 1 is long the token price. A success fee is the only revenue line in the category that goes up when models get cheaper.

The objection I expect is that developers will hate paying per PR. I think that is a vocabulary problem. Nobody minds that Fin charges per resolution, because the thing being charged for is the thing they wanted. A developer who objects to $1.50 for a merged PR is objecting to a number, not a meter, and the number is a tenth of what a credit-pool overage already costs the same person.

8. Limitations

  • Individuals only. The model has no teams or enterprise contracts, which were $2.6 billion of Cursor's reported revenue before the acquisition. Enterprise pooled usage changes the arithmetic on the heavy segment.
  • No competitive response. Churned developers vanish; they do not go to a competitor who then undercuts. In reality every regime's churn is bounded by what Copilot and Claude Code charge next month.
  • Willingness to pay is a guess. The segment anchors come from Cursor's own usage guidance and the $20 / $100 / $200 ladder, not from a survey. The relative ranking of regimes is robust to moving them; the absolute margins are not.
  • No cost of running the outcome meter. Attributing a merged PR to an agent needs tooling and a dispute process. I have not charged for either.
  • One vendor's cost structure. The 35% first-party cost ratio assumes the vendor owns its models. For a vendor that does not, the bundle's margin advantage disappears and the tariff looks relatively better.
